# TikTok research, part 2: what is INSIDE the streamer clips that break out (2026-11-21)

The first TikTok study ([TIKTOK_RESEARCH.md](TIKTOK_RESEARCH.md)) only had
numbers and captions, and found that captions barely matter. The owner then
asked: look at the actual videos, not the titles and captions. This study
does that, and focuses on streamer clips.

- **Tables:** `docs/research/tiktok_content_analysis.md` (re-generated by the script)
- **Per-video measures:** `docs/research/tiktok_content_measures_2026-11-21.jsonl`
- **Frame/sound numbers:** `docs/research/tiktok_frames_2026-11-21.jsonl.gz`;
  file facts: `docs/research/tiktok_file_facts_2026-11-21.jsonl.gz`
- **Blind labels:** `docs/research/tiktok_visual_labels_2026-11-21.json` (layout,
  captions, editing, first 3 seconds) and `tiktok_transcript_labels_2026-11-21.json`
- **Script:** `python -m src.clips.tiktok_content_research --labels docs/research/tiktok_visual_labels_2026-11-21.json`
  (`src/clips/tiktok_content_research.py`, tests in `tests/test_tiktok_content_research.py`)

## 1. Method

**The comparison.** For each account from the first study, its own videos
(7+ days old) were scored against that account's usual views for videos
posted within ±10 days. Three groups were picked per account:
- **hits:** 2x usual or more (top 10 for streamer-clip accounts);
- **flops:** 0.6x or less (bottom 10);
- **typical:** 4 from the middle.

Every finding compares an account's hits with **its own** flops, so follower
count, niche and house style cannot explain the difference. "Hits higher in
22 of 25 accounts" means 22 accounts' hits had the higher value.

**Sample:** 1,280 videos from 67 accounts. 624 of them (260 hits, 104 typical,
260 flops) are from 33 streamer-clip accounts.

**What was measured** (in the owner's logged-in Chrome, read-only; videos
were measured in memory and only numbers were kept, no video files):

| Source | What it gives |
|---|---|
| TikTok's own auto-transcript (timed lines) | when people talk, how fast, the first words, pauses, how it ends |
| TikTok's file facts | loudness reading, picture quality score, resolution, shape, original sound |
| 4 pictures per second, shrunk to 72x128 | cuts, movement, brightness, colour, still/sharp areas of the screen |
| Sound level every 0.1 s | loud moments, silence, and how loud the no-talking moments are |
| Claude watching, **blind** (group hidden, shuffled) | 42 clips × 12 stills (layout, captions, banner, editing, what happens); 60 clips' first 3 seconds; 104 transcripts (what kind of moment, is there a payoff) |

## 2. Findings (streamer clips)

### 2.1 Format does not decide it: each account looks the same in its hits and flops

In the blind review of 42 clips (11 accounts), each account's hits and flops
had the **same layout, caption style, title banner and editing**: split
facecam, blurred-bar horizontal, full-frame IRL, word-by-word captions or
none. Every account has a house style and uses it for everything. The
measured numbers agree:

| Measure | Hits | Flops | Hits higher / flops higher (accounts) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cuts per 10 s | 1.0 | 1.0 | 16 / 10 |
| Loudness (TikTok's reading) | -20.3 LUFS | -20.1 | 12 / 13 |
| Resolution | 1254 px | 1226 | 5 / 4 (mostly equal) |
| Shape (9:16 vs square) | 88% 9:16 | 88% | same |
| Movement between pictures | 13.1 | 12.6 | 14 / 12 |

TikTok's picture-quality score leaned towards hits (66.0 vs 63.9, 16 / 9),
but weakly. Small differences that leaned towards hits:
- fewer still areas of the screen (frozen backgrounds, bars): 5 / 17;
- a cut in the first 2 seconds: 13 / 5.

**Conclusion:** the layout, editing style and audio level we already produce
are not what separates a breakout from a flop.

### 2.2 The strongest signal: loud moments with no words (laughing, screaming, the crowd going off)

TikTok's auto-transcript only writes down words, so a laugh, a scream, a
crowd or game noise shows up as a gap. Matching those gaps with the sound
level separates **loud** gaps from **quiet** ones (dead air):

| Per 30 seconds | Hits | Typical | Flops | Hits higher / flops higher |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Loud wordless stretches (2 s+)** | **0.64** | 0.27 | 0.35 | **22 / 3** |
| Quiet wordless stretches (dead air) | 0.40 | 0.00 | 0.29 | 17 / 7 |
| Share of the clip with talking | 70% | 81% | 82% | 5 / 20 |
| Words per second while talking | 3.42 | 3.44 | 3.58 | 6 / 19 |

This held for 15-45 s clips only (17 / 3). Hits **talk less**. They have
moments where the reaction itself takes over and nobody speaks: laughter,
screaming, a crowd, or a stunned silence before the next line.
Flops are wall-to-wall talking.

The quiet stretches also lean towards hits (17 / 7). A beat of silence
before a reaction or answer seems to be part of the moment, not a fault.

**Replication on 1,209 fresh clips (same 26 accounts, not used above).**
Hits and flops here were all clips at 2x+ or 0.6x- usual views, not each
account's 10 most extreme. The result was weaker, and only part of it held:

| Fresh clips | Hits | Flops | Hits higher / flops higher |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share of the clip with talking (5x+ vs 0.3x-) | 77% | 83% | **4 / 20** |
| Share of the clip with no words (5x+ vs 0.3x-) | 14% | 8% | **17 / 6** |
| Loud wordless stretches per 30 s (2x+ vs 0.6x-) | 0.50 | 0.36 | 13 / 12 |
| Loud wordless stretches per 30 s (5x+ vs 0.3x-) | 0.53 | 0.32 | 14 / 9 |

So the robust lesson is **less talking, more room for the reaction**.
Whether that wordless time is *loud* is weaker evidence than the first
sample suggested (the 22 / 3 above was partly luck in a small sample).

### 2.3 Hits do not open on someone explaining

| First 3 seconds (blind, 60 clips) | Hits | Flops |
|---|---|---|
| Something already happening (action, reaction) | 45% | 32% |
| The scene just before it (nothing yet) | 38% | 23% |
| **Someone talking / setting it up** | **17%** | **45%** |

Measured over all 624 clips, hits had **fewer words in the first 3 s**
(9.6 vs 11.7; 7 / 18) and their first words came later (0.8 s vs 0.5 s).
The hits put you in the situation and let it play. The flops start with
someone explaining.

### 2.4 What is said does not decide it on its own, but who and when do

104 transcripts (2 hits + 2 flops from 26 accounts) were labelled blind:

| Moment type | Hits | Flops |
|---|---|---|
| Something happens (outcome, reveal, surprise) | 13 | 11 |
| Conflict / drama | 5 | 7 |
| Emotional / story | 5 | 4 |
| One-liner | 2 | 2 |
| Chat, encounter, other | 27 | 28 |
| *Clear payoff in the words* | **22** | 15 |

From the words alone, hits and flops were hard to tell apart; a clear
payoff leaned towards hits. After unblinding, many hits turned out to be tied to **the week's
story**: the Streamer Awards results, N3on being the only streamer in
GTA 6, Cole Palmer streaming on Twitch, the Wendy Ortiz fight, Ronaldo's
retirement. That matches part 1, where people/event names marked hits and
generic hype words marked flops.

**Being first seems to matter.** Among 141 pairs where two different clip
accounts posted the same moment within 3 days (captions sharing 40%+ of
their words), the earlier post did better 81 times. Within 6 hours:
61 of 102 pairs, with the earlier post at 1.45x its account's usual views
against 0.91x for the later one. This is suggestive (caption matching is
loose), but it fits the live recorder's advantage: we can post a moment
within the hour.

### 2.4a All watched clips together (3,636 streamer clips, 25-26 accounts)

`python -m src.clips.tiktok_watch_research` → `docs/research/tiktok_watch_analysis.md`.
Big breakouts (5x+ usual) against real flops (0.3x or less), each account
against itself:

| Measure | Breakouts | Flops | Accounts (hits higher / flops higher) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share of the clip with talking | 76% | 83% | 5 / **20** |
| Share with no words (2 s+ gaps) | 14% | 11% | **21** / 4 |
| Loud wordless stretches per 30 s | 0.58 | 0.34 | **18** / 7 |
| Quiet wordless stretches per 30 s | 0.41 | 0.29 | **18** / 6 |
| Words in the first 3 s | 11.0 | 12.3 | 6 / **19** |
| Time after the last words | 0.7 s | 0.4 s | **19** / 6 |
| Highest surprise on a face | 0.35 | 0.31 | 18 / 7 |
| Highest happiness on a face | 0.90 | 0.87 | 17 / 8 |
| Laughing-face moments per 30 s | 1.34 | 1.41 | 13 / 12 |

At the looser 2x / 0.6x split the same directions hold, but more weakly.

**Where the big moment sits.** The loudest second sits in the middle in both
groups (55% of the way through breakouts vs 51% for flops). Breakouts have
about 14 s of build-up before it and 15 s after it; flops are just shorter.
Give the moment its setup and its aftermath.

**Following up a hit.** A clip whose caption shares 25%+ of its words with a hit
the same account posted in the previous 5 days did only a little better:
1.13x usual (median) vs 0.97x. 28% of those follow-ups became hits (3x+),
against 26% of everything else (1,108 vs 3,607 clips, 26 accounts). So
riding a trend helps a little, but each moment still has to be good.

### 2.4a-2 Out-of-sample test: 80 clip accounts never seen before (5,043 clips)

New clip accounts found through user and video search (DUB, DDG, Adapt, Kai
Cenat, IShowSpeed, Adin Ross, N3on, Lacy, Marlon, Silky, StableRonaldo, Jynxzi,
Case-Oh, Kick and "stream moments" pages and others) were listed by scrolling
their profiles and watched the same way. Hits higher / flops higher, by account:

| Measure | 2x+ vs 0.6x (80 accounts) | 5x+ vs 0.3x (67 accounts) |
|---|---|---|
| Length | **56 / 24** (40 s vs 37 s) | **48 / 19** (42 s vs 34 s) |
| Highest surprise on a face | **51 / 29** | **43 / 24** (0.39 vs 0.35) |
| Share of pictures with 2+ faces | 38 / 42 | 39 / 28 (47% vs 43%) |
| Share of the clip with talking | 39 / 41 | 23 / **44** (73% vs 77%) |
| Loud wordless stretches | 35 / 43 | 34 / 32 (*not* replicated) |
| Time after the last words | 43 / 37 | 34 / 33 |
| Highest happiness on a face | 44 / 36 | 37 / 30 |
| Laughing-face moments per 30 s | 36 / **44** (flops lean) | 30 / 37 |
| Quiet wordless stretches | 44 / 36 | 34 / 33 |
| Words in the first 3 s | 39 / 40 | 30 / 37 |

As more accounts came in, only three patterns kept holding: **breakouts are
longer**, **a face looks more surprised**, and among the biggest breakouts
**more people are on screen and there is a little less talking**. Loud
wordless stretches looked strong in the first sample (22 of 25 accounts) but
are a tie here. Keep reaction seconds because cutting a laugh ruins the
moment, not because they predict views.

So among the *biggest* breakouts against real flops, the first sample's
picture comes back: less talking, more loud reaction moments, more people,
a bigger surprised face, and longer clips.

**Honest conclusion:** the talking, pause and loudness patterns are *small
tendencies* that show up in some account sets and not others. They are not
rules. The one finding that held in every sample is that **breakouts run
longer** (part 1 too). The rest of the difference is the moment itself:
what happens, who is in it and whether it is this week's story. The editor
model judges that from the transcript; these signals only point it at the
right seconds.

### 2.4b Watching for laughing and shocked faces (2,000+ more clips)

The watcher also read every face twice a second with the FER+ expression
model (`src/clips/expressions.py`), alongside the sound model and the transcript.

- **A laughing face alone does not make a clip.** Peak "happiness" first
  looked higher in hits (20 / 6 accounts after 197 clips). After 431 clips it
  was 15 / 11. Streamers smile and laugh constantly, in flops too. The model
  also scores plain smiles as happiness and open-mouth talking as surprise.
- **What caused the laugh matters** (60 laughs read blind, 30 per group):
  banter jokes were equally common (37% of hit laughs vs 43% of flop laughs)
  and polite or social laughs were more common in flops (33% vs 20%).
  Awkward moments (13% vs 0%), hype (13% vs 7%) and chaos (10% vs 7%) leaned
  towards hits.
- **What caused the shock matters** (40 shocked faces with nobody talking,
  stills read blind): a **real-life shock** was 42% of hit moments vs 10% of
  flop moments. That means a stunt, a prank, a scare, police, something
  breaking, a surprise arrival or a drama reveal. **In-game hype** (a win, a
  near-miss, a buzzer-beater) was 33% of flop moments vs 5% of hit moments.
- **The sound model misses most laughs.** On TikTok audio, CED-tiny rarely
  scored laughter or screaming above 0.3 (median peak laughter 0.07). It
  hears "music" almost everywhere. Loudness plus "no words" catches far more.
- **How clips end:** hits leave about 1 s after the last word (1.0 s vs 0.5 s;
  18 / 8). Our trimming already keeps 1 s, and up to 3 s while laughing.

- **Spoken words** (auto-transcripts; 927 clips at 3x+ usual vs 714 at 0.4x
  or less, words used in 6+ accounts). Clips are pooled across accounts, so
  these are themes, not measured effects:
  - *Breakout words* are real-world action and people: open (37 vs 7 clips),
    buy (30 vs 5), family, outside, award, week, song, fake, bet, message,
    Kai, Marlon, streamers, and body words (hands, head, eyes, touch).
  - *Flop words* are gaming and explaining: gta, kill, fight, winner,
    basically, story, reason, plus generic "holy", "funny" and "chat".
  This agrees with the shock finding: real-life events and other people beat
  in-game action and explanations.

Small blind samples; treat the percentages as directions.

### 2.4c Music-only clips and missing captions (8,000+ watched clips)

Our sound model tags each second as speech, music, laughter and so on. A clip
was called **music over a visual moment** when under 30% of its seconds had
talking and at least 60% had music. Examples: a streamer vibing to a song,
a short reveal set to a track, a meme-style edit.

| How far above the account's usual | Clips | Music over a visual moment |
|---|---|---|
| 0.3x or less (flops) | 1,683 | 11.8% |
| 0.3-0.6x | 2,575 | 11.7% |
| 0.6-2x | 1,497 | 12.5% |
| 2-5x | 1,831 | 11.4% |
| 5-20x | 1,479 | 11.6% |
| 20-100x | 693 | 10.8% |
| **100x or more** | **222** | **20.3%** |

So these clips are a long shot. They are not more likely to be a hit than a
talking clip, but they show up about twice as often among the giant breakouts.
Typical giants: Marlon vibing to a song, Kai Cenat's GTA reveal set to music,
Druski's reaction as famous guests walk in. ShortsEngine chooses moments from
the transcript, so it cannot find these yet. A "music moment" pick (the
streamer reacting or dancing while a song plays) would be the way to try it.

**Missing captions are a data caveat, not a lesson.** 29% of watched clips had
no TikTok caption track. That was more common among flops (36% vs 21%; 57 of
85 accounts). This happened even when the sound model heard talking. TikTok
probably makes captions after a video starts spreading, so this is an effect
of views, not a cause. The talking measures above use only clips that have
captions, so hits and flops are compared on the same footing.

### 2.5 Smaller notes

- Hits were a little longer (median 29 s vs 23 s; 16 / 10), in line with part 1.
- A gap after the last words (the reaction at the end) leaned towards hits
  (0.6 s vs 0.3 s; 17 / 8). Ending mid-sentence was equally common in both
  groups (25%), so it is not what makes a flop.
- Hits' loud parts stood out more from their normal level in 17 of 26 accounts
  (medians equal at 8 dB), and their loudest second was more often in the
  last third (40% vs 30%; 12 / 8).

## 3. What this suggests for ShortsEngine (for the owner to decide)

Nothing in the pipeline was changed.

1. **Stop punishing reaction gaps as "dead air".** `ranking.py` (pacing)
   counts every gap over 1.5 s without words as dead air. Hits have *more*
   of those gaps, the loud kind. Proposal: count only quiet gaps as dead
   air, and add a "reaction beats" bonus for loud wordless gaps or gaps
   where the sound model hears laughter/screaming. The sound model is
   already in `audio_events.py`.
2. **Prefer moments where the reaction takes over from the talking.**
   Give a small bonus to windows with 70% talking or less that include a
   loud reaction, and a small penalty to windows that are 90%+ non-stop talk.
3. **Start on the situation, not the explanation.** When choosing the start,
   prefer starting on the scene or the action. Avoid starting on a sentence
   that explains what is about to happen (the context line on screen does
   that job).
4. **Keep the format; spend effort on choosing moments and on speed.** Layout,
   captions and editing style did not separate hits from flops. Posting
   quickly and picking moments tied to this week's story (awards, a new
   game, a feud, a famous guest) looks more valuable.
5. **Test it ourselves.** The packaging experiment (`packaging.py`) can't
   test moment choice, but the decision log can: tag each posted clip with
   its loud-wordless count and opening type, then compare once 20+ clips
   are 48 h old.

## 4. Limits

- **Correlation, not cause**, as in part 1. Accounts may post their best
  moments in a particular way.
- **The auto-transcript is not perfect.** Shouting or fast crosstalk it
  could not transcribe also counts as "wordless". That still means the
  moment is carried by the reaction, not by clear speech.
- **The blind labels are one viewer's judgement** (Claude), on small samples
  (42, 60 and 104 clips). Treat the percentages as directions, not precise
  numbers.
- **Only first-language-English transcripts** were read; the numeric measures
  include every language.
- **No retention data:** TikTok shows watch time only to the account owner.
- Other genres: the transcript and file measures for 656 non-streamer
  videos are in the tables, but frames were measured only for streamer clips.
